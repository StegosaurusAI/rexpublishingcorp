<p>As of <strong>Wednesday, August 26, 2026</strong>, TRADUKI still says its next translation-grants submission window runs from <strong>July 1 to September 1, 2026</strong>. That leaves only a short filing window for publishers that already know the source language, target market, rights path, and translator. The useful point is not that funding exists. It is that TRADUKI's rules are narrow enough to expose very quickly whether a project is truly ready.</p>

<p>The live <a href="https://traduki.eu/grants/">TRADUKI grants page</a> and the current <a href="https://applications.traduki.eu/translation-guidelines">translation-support guidelines</a> describe a regional network built around German-language and Southeast European literary exchange, not a general European translation fund. That distinction matters because a large share of weak applications fail long before quality becomes the issue. They fail on route, paperwork, or expectations about how the money moves.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The September 1 deadline matters only if the route already qualifies</h2>

<p>TRADUKI's current rules support four lanes: translations from German into Albanian, Bosnian, Bulgarian, Croatian, Macedonian, Montenegrin, Romanian, Serbian, and Slovenian; translations from those languages into German; translations among those Southeast European languages; and translations of Swiss authors writing in Italian, French, and Rhaeto-Romansh into that Southeast European set.</p>

<p>That is a workable network, but it is not broad cultural-export language. A publisher cannot treat TRADUKI as a fallback for any interesting cross-border book. If the project sits outside those routes, the application is not almost eligible. It is in the wrong programme.</p>

<p>The same guidelines also say only <strong>direct translations from the original language</strong> qualify. That closes off another common shortcut. If the project depends on a relay translation, the live rules do not support it.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The 8,000-euro ceiling is not translation money alone</h2>

<p>TRADUKI says the maximum grant is <strong>8,000 euros</strong>, including both the <strong>translation fee</strong> and the <strong>license fee</strong>. That is more useful than a vague "up to" announcement because it forces cleaner budgeting. A publisher has to model the translator payment and the rights cost together instead of pretending the grant covers one side cleanly and the other side later.</p>

<p>That combined ceiling also makes project selection stricter. A book with expensive rights, a long manuscript, or a demanding specialist translator may still be worth doing, but it may not fit this fund comfortably. Readers should treat TRADUKI as a bounded workflow tool, not as proof that every desirable project can be made viable inside one grant cap.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The payment structure changes the normal publishing workflow</h2>

<p>The guidelines say TRADUKI normally pays the <strong>translation fee directly to the translator</strong> and the agreed <strong>license fee directly to the rights holder</strong>. For translations into German, however, payment is handled differently: the applicant is paid after publication and after it has already paid the translator and copyright proprietor.</p>

<p>That difference matters operationally. For most supported routes, the fund is not simply reimbursing the publisher after the fact. It sits more directly inside the payment chain. But for German-target translations, the applicant still needs the cash discipline to bridge the project first. A team that misses that distinction can misread its own financing exposure.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The exclusions are narrow enough to save time before the form opens</h2>

<p>TRADUKI says excluded works include specialist or purely academic publications, secondary literature, textbooks, self-help books, titles that already received translation support from other sources, titles dealing with natural sciences, self-translations, previously rejected applications, and books spreading hate speech. It also limits support to books not yet translated into the target language.</p>

<p>That means the pre-application check should be blunt. If the project is academically specialized, already funded elsewhere, or already translated into the target language, the strongest move is to stop early and preserve time for a better-fit submission. The September 1 deadline is only useful when the file is genuinely admissible.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What publishers should have ready before they spend the final week on TRADUKI</h2>

<ol class="mb-6 list-decimal pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Confirm the language architecture.</strong> The project should fit one of TRADUKI's explicit German and Southeast Europe routes.</li>
  <li><strong>Check the translation path.</strong> The live rules require a direct translation from the original language.</li>
  <li><strong>Model the money honestly.</strong> The <strong>8,000-euro</strong> ceiling covers both translation and license fees, not translation alone.</li>
  <li><strong>Understand who gets paid.</strong> In most approved cases TRADUKI pays the translator and rights holder directly, while translations into German work on a post-publication reimbursement basis.</li>
  <li><strong>Assemble the dossier before the last day.</strong> The guidelines require the signed copyright contract, option, or letter of intent, the translator's CV and bibliography, and a backlist list for the past two years.</li>
</ol>
