<p>The U.S. Copyright Office's <a href="https://copyright.gov/newsnet/2026/1090.html">July 14, 2026 notice</a> is useful because it turns an abstract rulemaking into a near-term workflow question. The Office says it has submitted a proposed new fee schedule to Congress, says this would be its first fee change since 2020, and says it seeks to implement the new fees in fall 2026 unless Congress disapproves the schedule during the 120-day review window.</p>

<p>For Rex readers, the practical issue is not whether every filing should suddenly be rushed. The practical issue is <strong>which routine filings are exposed to meaningful cost changes, which ones are not, and whether any registration or recordation work is worth finishing before the proposed schedule can take effect</strong>.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What actually changes in the proposal</h2>

<p>The Copyright Office's fee-study page says the final July 2026 proposal covers commonly used services including registration and recordation. The detailed <a href="https://www.copyright.gov/rulemaking/feestudy2026/proposed-fee-schedule.pdf">proposed fee schedule</a> shows the lines most likely to matter to authors, agents, estates, and small publishing teams:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Single electronic application:</strong> proposed to rise from <code>$45</code> to <code>$55</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Other electronic registration applications:</strong> proposed to rise from <code>$65</code> to <code>$85</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Paper registration:</strong> proposed to rise from <code>$125</code> to <code>$185</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Group Registration of Unpublished Works (GRUW):</strong> proposed to rise from <code>$85</code> to <code>$130</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Group Registration of Short Online Literary Works (GRTX):</strong> proposed to rise from <code>$65</code> to <code>$130</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Electronic recordation base fee:</strong> proposed to rise from <code>$95</code> to <code>$215</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Most paper recordations:</strong> proposed to rise from <code>$125</code> to <code>$350</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Paper notices of termination:</strong> proposed to rise from <code>$125</code> to <code>$275</code>.</li>
</ul>

<p>That is enough to justify attention, especially for teams handling assignments, reversions, or older-contract cleanup. But the same table also keeps this from becoming a lazy <em>everything got more expensive</em> story. The Office proposes that <strong>electronic supplementary registration would fall from <code>$100</code> to <code>$85</code></strong>, which means the operational takeaway has to stay specific.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Registration and recordation are not the same job</h2>

<p>Readers can also get turned around here because the proposal touches two different workflows. The Office's <a href="https://www.copyright.gov/registration/">registration portal</a> remains the live hub for literary-works registration, including the common one-work path as well as group options such as GRTX and GRUW. The Office's <a href="https://www.copyright.gov/recordation/">recordation overview</a> separately explains that recordation covers transfers of copyright ownership, other documents pertaining to copyright, and notices of termination.</p>

<p>That distinction matters because a novelist registering a finished manuscript, a small press recording an assignment, and an estate preparing a termination filing are not solving the same problem. They are just being affected by the same proposed fee reset.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Where the planning pressure is actually strongest</h2>

<p>The proposed changes do not hit every Rex reader equally. The modest move on the single electronic application matters, but it is not the real budget shock in this package. The bigger pressure points are the workflows that already feel procedural or back-office heavy and are easy to postpone:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Backlogged registration work.</strong> If you have finished works that were already headed for filing, the difference between doing that work before or after a fall 2026 fee change is now worth pricing explicitly.</li>
  <li><strong>Group filing decisions.</strong> Teams using GRUW or GRTX for batches of unpublished or short online material should revisit whether the batch is actually ready instead of letting it drift.</li>
  <li><strong>Recordation cleanup.</strong> The jump in proposed recordation fees is large enough that old assignments, grants, and rights-chain paperwork should stop being treated as someday admin.</li>
  <li><strong>Termination planning.</strong> If a notice of termination is already on counsel's or an agent's calendar, this is a good moment to check whether paperwork, signatures, and timing are being allowed to slip.</li>
</ul>

<p>The point is not to panic-file. The point is to stop pretending the fee question is irrelevant until the Office sets an exact implementation date.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What changed after comments, and why that matters</h2>

<p>The Office's July 14 notice says the final proposal reflects modifications made after stakeholder feedback. One practical example is the single application. The Office did <strong>not</strong> eliminate that lane. Instead, it kept the form and proposed a smaller increase to <code>$55</code>. That matters because many individual authors still rely on the single-application path for straightforward filings.</p>

<p>That nuance is worth preserving in the article because it tells readers the Office is not simply closing off lower-cost options. It is re-pricing them while trying to recover more of its costs.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What Rex readers should do now</h2>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>Separate registration, supplementary registration, recordation, and termination work into different buckets before you estimate cost exposure.</li>
  <li>Pull forward filings that are already decision-ready instead of rushing work that is still factually or contractually messy.</li>
  <li>Use the current registration and recordation pages to confirm the right filing lane before budgeting against the proposed fees.</li>
  <li>Keep the dates explicit: the schedule was proposed on <strong>July 14, 2026</strong>, and the Office says it seeks implementation in <strong>fall 2026</strong> after the statutory congressional window.</li>
  <li>Escalate to qualified counsel when a filing decision turns on ownership disputes, termination eligibility, or deal interpretation rather than simple timing.</li>
</ul>

<p>The useful lesson here is procedural. A proposed federal fee schedule does not automatically force action. But it does change the economics of delay. For authors, agents, and small publishing teams with real filing work still sitting in draft folders, that is enough reason to review the queue now rather than after the new schedule is already in force.</p>
