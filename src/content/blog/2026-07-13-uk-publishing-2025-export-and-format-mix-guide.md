<p>When trade bodies publish annual market figures, the temptation is to treat one headline number like a verdict on the whole business. That is not the most useful way to read the UK Publishers Association's latest release.</p>

<p>On <strong>3 June 2026</strong>, the Publishers Association published <a href="https://www.publishers.org.uk/publications/publishing-in-2025/"><em>Publishing in 2025</em></a> and a matching summary headlined <a href="https://www.publishers.org.uk/publishing-reaches-highest-ever-revenue-in-2025/">"Publishing reaches highest ever revenue in 2025."</a> The topline is straightforward: the association says total UK publishing revenue reached <strong>GBP7.4 billion</strong> in 2025, up <strong>3%</strong> year over year.</p>

<p>The more practical lesson for Rex readers is not simply that revenue hit a record. It is that <strong>export dependence remains heavy</strong>, <strong>digital is still growing faster than print</strong>, and <strong>format choices are diverging by sector</strong> rather than moving in one clean direction.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Exports are still doing much of the work</h2>

<p>The Publishers Association says export revenue reached <strong>GBP4.7 billion</strong> in 2025, up <strong>4%</strong>, while the home market reached <strong>GBP2.6 billion</strong>, up <strong>3%</strong>. It also names the <strong>United States, Australia, and Germany</strong> as the largest export markets across academic, education, and consumer publishing.</p>

<p>That matters because it keeps the operational focus where many smaller teams need it most. In an export-facing business, growth is not only about domestic sell-through. It is also about territorial reach, rights sales, licensing strategy, format readiness, and whether metadata and distribution workflows can support more than one market cleanly.</p>

<p>For authors, agents, and rights holders, that means the UK figures are best read as a reminder that international revenue is not a side lane. It is core trade infrastructure.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Digital grew faster, but print still held the center of gravity</h2>

<p>The same Publishers Association release says total print revenue was <strong>GBP3.8 billion</strong> in 2025, essentially flat year over year, while digital revenue reached <strong>GBP3.6 billion</strong>, up <strong>7%</strong>. That is a meaningful shift, but it is not the same thing as a print collapse.</p>

<p>In consumer publishing, the distinction is even sharper. The association says consumer print revenue reached <strong>GBP2.1 billion</strong>, up <strong>2%</strong>, and still accounted for <strong>79%</strong> of consumer revenue. Consumer digital revenue reached <strong>GBP558 million</strong>, up <strong>7%</strong>, while digital audiobooks reached <strong>GBP255 million</strong>, up <strong>10%</strong>.</p>

<p>The clean reading is that digital formats are gaining ground, especially audio, but print remains the dominant commercial format in the consumer market. That is a planning signal, not a contradiction.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Do not underinvest in print packaging and availability</strong> just because digital growth rates look faster.</li>
  <li><strong>Do not treat audio growth as universal</strong> if your category, territory, or rights position is weak.</li>
  <li><strong>Do build format plans deliberately</strong> if you expect a title to travel across export markets or sales channels.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The sector split matters more than one industry headline</h2>

<p>The Publishers Association figures are most useful when they are separated by sector. Consumer publishing was up <strong>3%</strong> overall, with <strong>fiction up 8%</strong>, <strong>children's up 7%</strong>, and <strong>non-fiction down 3%</strong>. Education publishing fell to <strong>GBP636 million</strong>, down <strong>4%</strong>. Academic publishing, by contrast, reached <strong>GBP3.7 billion</strong>, up <strong>5%</strong>, with export growth doing much of the work there as well.</p>

<p>That spread is a good warning against lazy market talk. A format or rights strategy that looks reasonable in fiction may not travel into education. A home-market slowdown in one segment can sit alongside strong export performance in another. One annual total cannot tell you where your own list is actually strong or exposed.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What Rex readers should actually pull from these figures</h2>

<p>This is a UK trade-body dataset, not a Europe-wide census and not a forecast. The safest use of it is operational.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Rights teams:</strong> keep export-market assumptions explicit. If international revenue is part of the business case, title materials and territorial positioning need to be ready earlier.</li>
  <li><strong>Authors and agents:</strong> ask which formats are truly planned, not merely theoretically available. Growth in digital and audio does not guarantee that every title will be produced or distributed well in those formats.</li>
  <li><strong>Publishing operations teams:</strong> test format priorities against category reality. Faster digital growth does not remove the need for disciplined print execution.</li>
  <li><strong>Small presses:</strong> treat market data as a budgeting tool, not a mood board. Sector divergence is real, so one generic "the market is up" story is not enough to build on.</li>
</ul>

<p>The broader lesson is calm but important. UK publishing's 2025 figures do support a growth story. They also show that the growth is uneven, export-reliant, and format-specific. That is the version worth using when real rights, production, and investment decisions are on the table.</p>
