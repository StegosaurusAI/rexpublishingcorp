<p>WIPO's <a href="https://www.wipo.int/en/web/abc/w/news/2026/marrakesh-treaty-assembly-kicks-off-10th-anniversary-celebrations-at-wipo">July 2026 update on the Marrakesh Treaty Assembly and the Accessible Books Consortium</a> is useful because it keeps one important accessibility question grounded in actual infrastructure. As of <strong>Thursday, July 23, 2026</strong>, the clearest signal is not that the treaty exists. It is that cross-border accessible-book exchange is getting broader, more multilingual, and a little easier to use, while still depending on real implementation work.</p>

<p>WIPO says the Marrakesh Treaty Assembly met in Geneva on <strong>July 13, 2026</strong> and marked the start of the treaty's tenth-anniversary celebrations. It also says <strong>Albania, Angola, Cuba, and Mozambique</strong> joined since the prior session, while <strong>Niger</strong> announced from the floor that it was ready to deposit its instrument of accession. That is useful movement, but it is not the whole story.</p>

<p>For Rex readers, the practical question is narrower. If you work in rights, accessibility, or international publishing operations, what does this update change in day-to-day terms? The answer is not "everything." It is that the legal framework is spreading, the delivery tools are improving, and teams should stop treating accessible-book exchange as either a solved problem or a purely symbolic one.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The March and July numbers should be read together</h2>

<p>WIPO's formal <a href="https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/govbody/en/mvt_a_11/mvt_a_11_1.pdf">May 5, 2026 status document</a> says that as of <strong>March 30, 2026</strong> the Marrakesh Treaty had <strong>103 Contracting Parties</strong> covering <strong>129 WIPO Member States</strong>. The July assembly update adds the newer political signal: four new Contracting Parties since the last session, plus Niger's statement that it was ready to deposit its instrument.</p>

<p>Those dates matter. The March 30 count is the formal baseline presented to the assembly. The July 13 update is the current checkpoint. Read together, they show continued expansion. Read sloppily, they turn into a fuzzy headline about universal access that the evidence does not support.</p>

<p>The safer interpretation is simple. More countries are joining the framework, which improves the conditions for cross-border exchange. But treaty participation still is not the same thing as frictionless implementation inside every national market, library workflow, or authorized-entity network.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The ABC catalogue is now big enough to matter as an operations story</h2>

<p>This is where WIPO's update becomes more concrete. It says the <strong>ABC Global Book Service</strong> now contains <strong>over 1.2 million accessible books</strong> in <strong>80 languages</strong>, all available free of charge for cross-border exchange under the treaty framework.</p>

<p>That is not just a ceremonial statistic. It means the conversation has moved beyond a thin pilot phase. A multilingual catalogue at that scale changes what libraries, authorized entities, and accessibility-minded publishers should ask next: how much of the content readers actually need is discoverable, how smoothly requests move across borders, and where local bottlenecks still slow delivery down.</p>

<p>WIPO also says direct user access is available through ABC's <strong>Beneficiary Application</strong> and the <strong>Dolphin EasyReader App</strong>. That matters because the treaty can feel abstract until a user can actually search, find, and download an accessible title on a real device. The tools do not erase every access gap, but they do push the system closer to ordinary use rather than exceptional handling.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Publishers should treat this as an access workflow, not a legal talking point</h2>

<p>WIPO's <a href="https://www.wipo.int/en/web/abc">ABC overview</a> is blunt about the goal: increase the number of accessible books worldwide and make them available to people who are blind, have low vision, or are otherwise print disabled. That is the right frame for publishers too.</p>

<p>The treaty matters because it makes cross-border production and transfer easier. The ABC matters because it gives that legal framework an operating layer. What still matters on the publishing side is whether files, metadata, rights understanding, and partner handoffs are clean enough for accessible editions to move where readers need them.</p>

<p>That is also why it helps to stop collapsing three different questions into one:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Has the country joined the treaty?</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Can authorized entities exchange books effectively under that framework?</strong></li>
  <li><strong>Can print-disabled readers actually reach the material through workable tools and local implementation?</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>Those are related questions, but they are not the same question. The July update is encouraging mainly because it moves all three in the right direction without pretending they have already converged everywhere.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The education number is another sign this is no longer theoretical</h2>

<p>WIPO says ABC partner organizations have produced <strong>over 24,000 educational books</strong> in accessible formats since 2015. That figure matters because it shows the consortium is not only building a catalogue. It is also supporting the creation of material that readers, students, and institutions can use.</p>

<p>For publishing teams, that makes the broader lesson harder to ignore. Accessible publishing is not only a compliance or reputation issue. It is a distribution and access issue with real downstream consequences for discovery, education, and readership across borders.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What Rex readers should do with this update</h2>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>Use <strong>March 30, 2026</strong> as the formal treaty-status baseline and <strong>July 13, 2026</strong> as the fresher assembly checkpoint.</li>
  <li>Do not treat Niger's announcement as completed accession until the instrument is actually deposited.</li>
  <li>Map accessible-book planning to real delivery tools, including authorized-entity exchange and direct-user channels where available.</li>
  <li>Keep file quality, metadata, and rights clarity in scope; a larger treaty footprint does not fix weak production handoffs by itself.</li>
  <li>Watch the ABC catalogue growth as an operating signal, not just a celebratory number.</li>
</ul>

<p>The useful takeaway is straightforward. WIPO's July 2026 Marrakesh update shows that cross-border accessible-book exchange is getting more real, not less. But the part that matters for publishers is still the same part that usually matters: whether the infrastructure, files, and handoffs are good enough for readers to feel the improvement.</p>
