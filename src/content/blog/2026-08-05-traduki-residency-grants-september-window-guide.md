<p>As of <strong>Wednesday, August 19, 2026</strong>, TRADUKI's <a href="https://traduki.eu/grants/">official grants page</a> still shows the next <strong>residency-grants submission window</strong> as open from <strong>August 15 to September 15, 2026</strong>. The live <a href="https://applications.traduki.eu/forms/residency/">application page</a> is even more direct: the <strong>deadline for submitting applications is September 15th, 2026</strong>. For Rex readers, the useful question is not whether the residency network sounds impressive. It is whether the applicant's geography, project type, and work plan already match the programme's actual rules.</p>

<p>That matters because TRADUKI is not offering one generic cultural mobility grant. Its <a href="https://applications.traduki.eu/residency-guidelines">guidelines</a> split the programme into several formats with different expectations, durations, and support mechanics. A translator under contract, a publisher seeking practical exchange, and a writer planning a research trip are not applying for the same thing just because the application form sits in one place.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The first filter is geography, and TRADUKI is exact about it</h2>

<p>The application page says eligible applicants are <strong>writers, translators, and book professionals</strong> from <strong>Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Croatia, Kosovo, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Romania, Serbia,</strong> and <strong>Slovenia</strong>. The guidelines repeat the same country list.</p>

<p>That is the first reason this deadline should be read carefully rather than shared loosely. TRADUKI's broader network connects Southeast Europe with the German-speaking literary world, but the live residency call is not framed as a universal international opportunity. If the applicant is outside the stated countries, the right reaction is not optimism. It is to stop and check eligibility before spending time on the packet.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">This is a six-format programme, not one residency with six labels</h2>

<p>TRADUKI's guidelines list <strong>translation residency</strong>, <strong>writing residency</strong>, <strong>interdisciplinary cooperation residency</strong>, <strong>practical exchange residency</strong>, <strong>research trip</strong>, and <strong>virtual residency</strong>. That structure is the practical center of the story.</p>

<p>The translation residency is for translators who already have <strong>a project under contract</strong> and need focused time, plus possible access to libraries, archives, writers' associations, or publishers in the host country. Writing residencies are for authors working on their own texts. Practical exchange residencies are broader and more operational: the guidelines say they are aimed at <strong>booksellers, publishers, editors, librarians, journalists and critics, archivists, literary agents, managers of literature houses, festival managers and staff, literature lecturers, literature scouts including translators</strong>, and similar book professionals.</p>

<p>That last category is especially relevant for Rex readers because it means the programme is not only for solitary writing time. A scout, editor, or publishing professional may have a real use case here if the application can show why a placement or visiting programme in Southeast Europe will produce a clear professional outcome.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The host map is concrete, and one city has a special limit</h2>

<p>TRADUKI's guidelines list partner locations in <strong>Belgrade, Bucharest, Novo Mesto, Podgorica, Prishtina, Sarajevo, Skopje, Split, Sofia,</strong> and <strong>Tirana</strong>. The same page notes one important exception: <strong>Podgorica hosts two-week residencies only</strong>.</p>

<p>That is a small detail, but it tells applicants how to think about the whole opportunity. This is not a vague promise that the network will figure something out later. The programme already has partner locations, residency lengths, and format definitions. If a proposed project needs six uninterrupted weeks, not every location will fit. If it needs several institutional visits across one country, the research-trip route may make more sense than a residential stay.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Support is real, but it changes by format and duration</h2>

<p>TRADUKI's guidelines say translation and writing residencies can run for <strong>2, 4, or 6 weeks</strong>. For those formats, the fellowship covers <strong>travel costs, accommodation, and subsistence</strong>, with stated support levels of <strong>500 euro for 2 weeks</strong>, <strong>1,000 euro for 4 weeks</strong>, and <strong>1,250 euro for 6 weeks</strong>.</p>

<p>Interdisciplinary cooperation residencies cover travel and accommodation for the visiting artist plus <strong>250 euro per week per person</strong> in subsistence. Practical exchange residencies cover <strong>travel costs, accommodation, and subsistence at 250 euro per week</strong>. Research trips cover <strong>travel and accommodation cost up to 750 euro per week</strong> plus <strong>250 euro per week</strong> in subsistence. Virtual residencies include <strong>250 euro per week per person</strong>.</p>

<p>The obvious planning lesson is that no applicant should treat "TRADUKI residency" as one budget line. The support model depends on what kind of work is being proposed. A translator under contract, a literary scout seeking partner meetings, and a collaborative digital project will each need a different expectations check before filing.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The packet is simple on paper and unforgiving in practice</h2>

<p>The guidelines say the application must include a <strong>biography or CV</strong>, a <strong>bibliography or artistic portfolio</strong> for writers and translators, and a <strong>plan of work or detailed project description</strong>. The live form also asks for a <strong>project description</strong>, an <strong>expected outcome</strong>, location preferences, and month availability.</p>

<p>That is a compact packet, but it still forces a decision. A strong application has to explain not only who the applicant is, but why the chosen format fits the project better than the alternatives. If the proposal cannot answer why this should be a translation residency instead of a research trip, or why a virtual residency is necessary instead of an in-person one, the weakness is probably in the project design rather than the prose.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What applicants should do before Tuesday, September 15, 2026</h2>

<ol class="mb-6 list-decimal pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Check country eligibility first.</strong> The live call is limited to applicants from the listed Southeast European countries.</li>
  <li><strong>Choose the residency type before polishing the narrative.</strong> TRADUKI's six formats are materially different, and the wrong fit will be obvious.</li>
  <li><strong>Match duration to the real work.</strong> A two-week stay, a six-week translation period, and a virtual collaboration should not be pitched as interchangeable.</li>
  <li><strong>Use the host-city list operationally.</strong> The partner map is part of the planning logic, not background decoration.</li>
  <li><strong>Write the expected outcome like a publishing workflow.</strong> TRADUKI is supporting mobility and exchange, not abstract aspiration.</li>
</ol>
