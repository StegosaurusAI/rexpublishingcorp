<p>Literature Ireland's <a href="https://www.literatureireland.com/grants/translation-grant-programme/">live translation-grant page</a> is useful because it removes one common rights-team fantasy: the idea that a translation subsidy can be handled whenever the rest of the deal is ready. As of <strong>Friday, July 17, 2026</strong>, the page says the 2026 application windows were <strong>January 27 to February 17</strong>, <strong>May 12 to May 27</strong>, and <strong>September 8 to September 29, 2026</strong>, and it adds that only applications made during the relevant window will be accepted unless prior permission is granted.</p>

<p>That means the first two windows are already gone. For anyone licensing Irish literature into another language this summer, the only live 2026 opportunity left is the <strong>September 8 to September 29, 2026</strong> window.</p>

<p>For Rex readers, that makes this less of a culture-funding story and more of a workflow story. The real question is not whether the programme exists. The real question is whether the rights deal, translation sample, and publication schedule are ready to survive a system that is explicit about timing, review, and post-publication proof.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The January 2026 rule change matters before you budget the project</h2>

<p>The same page now says that <strong>from January 2026</strong>, eligibility for the Literature Ireland Translation Grant is limited to literary works that are <strong>currently in copyright</strong>. It also says support for public-domain translation projects will be <strong>very limited</strong> because of pressure on the current translation-grant budget.</p>

<p>That is a meaningful filter. It does not mean every in-copyright book is a fit, and it does not mean public-domain work is impossible in every case. It does mean teams should stop treating this programme as a broad catch-all for any Irish work they happen to like. Before anyone worries about filing mechanics, they should confirm that the book is an eligible in-copyright project and that the rights path is commercially real.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Why the translation sample is not a minor attachment</h2>

<p>Literature Ireland says all applicants must submit a <strong>translation sample</strong> for independent expert assessment. That is one of the most useful signals on the page because it tells publishers what kind of workflow this really is. This is not a form you complete after a rights deal and a rough budget. It is a programme that expects editorial seriousness early.</p>

<p>For smaller houses, that usually means four things have to happen in order:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Choose the book early enough to act.</strong> A late scouting decision leaves too little room for sample preparation.</li>
  <li><strong>Identify the translator before the filing window opens.</strong> A weak or rushed sample is the wrong place to improvise.</li>
  <li><strong>Build the sample into the deal timeline.</strong> The sample is part of the application, not a downstream extra.</li>
  <li><strong>Treat the September window as a real deadline.</strong> If the packet is not ready by early September, 2026 may be over for this programme.</li>
</ul>

<p>The page also says decisions are usually communicated within <strong>six to eight weeks</strong>. That timeline is not long by institutional-funding standards, but it is long enough to break a hurried publication plan.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The no-printing rule is where rushed schedules fail</h2>

<p>Literature Ireland states that the book <strong>must not go to print before a decision has been made</strong> on the translation-grant application. That line matters more than any abstract enthusiasm about supporting Irish literature abroad.</p>

<p>If a foreign publisher is already pushing catalog copy, locking a seasonal print slot, or promising a publication month before the decision window is complete, the grant stops being a helpful support tool and starts becoming a scheduling risk. The September 2026 filing window plus a typical six-to-eight-week decision cycle points many projects toward a late-2026 or early-2027 publication reality, not an impulsive autumn launch.</p>

<p>That does not make the programme slow. It makes it honest. The practical lesson is that the grant should shape the production schedule at the start, not be squeezed into a timetable that was built without it.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Payment finishes after publication, not at the award stage</h2>

<p>The page is also clear about what happens after a successful award. Literature Ireland says publishers must provide an approved acknowledgment page, <strong>proof of payment to the translator</strong>, and copies of the published work before grant payment is completed.</p>

<p>That post-publication sequence matters because it changes how a publisher should model cash flow and internal admin. Winning the grant is not the end of the process. The publisher still has to:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>keep the acknowledgment wording compliant,</li>
  <li>pay the translator in a documentable way,</li>
  <li>deliver the required copies after publication, and</li>
  <li>treat grant payment as a controlled final step rather than instant reimbursement.</li>
</ul>

<p>For lean teams, that is a useful warning. A translation grant can reduce cost pressure without removing the need for orderly contracts, documentation, and production follow-through.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">This is an established export workflow, not a one-off call</h2>

<p>Literature Ireland's <a href="https://www.literatureireland.com/grants/monthly-library-update/">Monthly Library Update archive</a> adds helpful context. The archive says the programme has housed copies of awarded books for <strong>30 years</strong>. That should not be read as nostalgia. It should be read as evidence that publishers are dealing with a longstanding institutional export workflow, not a temporary campaign page.</p>

<p>That stability is useful, but it does not remove the need for precision. The safest reading is simple: the programme is established, the current rules are specific, and the remaining 2026 opening is narrow.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What Rex readers should do now</h2>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>Work from the exact remaining 2026 filing window: <strong>September 8 to September 29, 2026</strong>.</li>
  <li>Confirm the book is <strong>currently in copyright</strong> before budgeting time around the grant.</li>
  <li>Build the translation sample early enough for serious review, not as a last-minute attachment.</li>
  <li>Do not put the book into print before the grant decision lands.</li>
  <li>Plan for the post-publication paperwork, especially acknowledgment approval and translator-payment proof.</li>
</ul>

<p>The practical lesson is blunt. Literature Ireland's grant is most useful when a foreign publisher treats it as a narrow, document-heavy rights workflow with one remaining 2026 window, not as rolling support that can be dropped into the schedule whenever the team gets around to it.</p>
