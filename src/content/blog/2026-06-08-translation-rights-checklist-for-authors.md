<p>If you want to discuss translation rights seriously, the first job is not outreach. It is cleanup.</p>

<p>Too many rights conversations stall because the author cannot answer basic questions about ownership, prior grants, formats, or territories. That does not always kill a deal, but it does slow trust. A cleaner packet gives an agent, scout, translator, or rights buyer something concrete to evaluate.</p>

<p>This is the working checklist to finish before you start those conversations.</p>

<blockquote>
  <p>This is practical publishing guidance, not legal advice. Contract interpretation, reversion, moral rights, tax, and cross-border enforcement can vary by jurisdiction and should go to qualified counsel.</p>
</blockquote>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">1) Confirm who actually controls the rights</h2>

<p>Start with the unglamorous part: what rights do you own today, and what rights have you already granted away?</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.copyright.gov/what-is-copyright/">U.S. Copyright Office</a> notes that copyright owners hold exclusive rights and can authorize others to use them. <a href="https://www.wipo.int/en/web/copyright/">WIPO</a> also frames copyright as a bundle of economic and moral rights, with translation and adaptation commonly included in that bundle.</p>

<p>Before you pitch translation rights, pull together:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>the current author or rights-holder name</li>
  <li>any co-author, illustrator, or contributor agreements</li>
  <li>work-made-for-hire terms, if any</li>
  <li>assignment documents</li>
  <li>agent agreements</li>
  <li>publisher contracts</li>
  <li>reversion clauses or termination rights</li>
  <li>prior licenses for translation, audio, ebook, print, serialization, or adaptation</li>
  <li>permissions for third-party material such as photos, maps, song lyrics, or long quotations</li>
</ul>

<p>If you cannot say clearly who controls the rights, fix that first.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">2) Write down the scope of the deal you are willing to discuss</h2>

<p>“Translation rights” is too vague on its own. A usable rights conversation needs scope.</p>

<p>At minimum, define your position on:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Language:</strong> which language or languages are available?</li>
  <li><strong>Territory:</strong> worldwide, regional, or country-specific?</li>
  <li><strong>Term:</strong> how long would the grant last?</li>
  <li><strong>Exclusivity:</strong> exclusive or nonexclusive?</li>
  <li><strong>Sublicensing:</strong> allowed or not?</li>
  <li><strong>Formats:</strong> print, ebook, audiobook, enhanced ebook, app, large print, other digital variants?</li>
  <li><strong>Adaptation rights:</strong> only translation, or also abridgment, dramatization, educational adaptation, or other derivative uses?</li>
</ul>

<p>This matters because cross-border licensing is not just about the text. <a href="https://www.wipo.int/en/web/copyright/activities/copyright_licensing">WIPO’s licensing guidance</a> points out that digital technology affects the territorial and temporal framework for licensing and that cross-border licensing disputes are common enough to require careful handling.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">3) Separate language rights from format rights</h2>

<p>One routine mistake is treating “foreign rights” as a single yes-or-no field. In practice, language and format often need separate handling.</p>

<p>A buyer may want:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>Polish-language print only</li>
  <li>Spanish-language ebook only</li>
  <li>German-language audiobook rights</li>
  <li>world French rights excluding Canada</li>
  <li>English audio rights in Europe only</li>
</ul>

<p>That is why clean metadata helps. <a href="https://www.editeur.org/83/Overview/">EDItEUR’s ONIX framework</a> is built to communicate book product metadata across trading partners, and its codelists support precise handling of language, country, and product form.</p>

<p>If your records are messy, at least create a plain-language rights table that lists:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Language:</strong> available, granted, or restricted languages</li>
  <li><strong>Territory:</strong> countries included or excluded</li>
  <li><strong>Format:</strong> print, ebook, audiobook, app, other</li>
  <li><strong>Term:</strong> start date, end date, renewal rules</li>
  <li><strong>Exclusivity:</strong> exclusive or nonexclusive</li>
  <li><strong>Sublicensing:</strong> permitted, restricted, or prohibited</li>
  <li><strong>Status:</strong> available, under option, already licensed, reverted</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">4) Prepare the metadata packet before anyone asks for it</h2>

<p>A rights conversation moves faster when the packet is ready on day one.</p>

<p>Keep these items together:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>final title and subtitle</li>
  <li>author name and short bio</li>
  <li>word count or page count</li>
  <li>publication date and publication status</li>
  <li>synopsis</li>
  <li>target readership</li>
  <li>comparable market positioning without hype</li>
  <li>sample chapters or excerpt</li>
  <li>translation sample if one exists</li>
  <li>sales history and notable endorsements, if real and relevant</li>
  <li>awards or media coverage, if real and relevant</li>
  <li>rights contact and decision-maker details</li>
</ul>

<p>Do not make the other side guess whether you are selling a finished product, a proposal, or a moving target.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">5) Get format identifiers straight</h2>

<p>Even if you are still early, you should know how many actual products you may be licensing.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.isbn-international.org/content/guidelines-assignment-e-books/26">International ISBN Agency’s guidance on e-books</a> says each separate digital publication may need its own ISBN when differences in format, DRM, platform dependence, or usage constraints make it a distinct product. In plain English: one book can turn into multiple commercially distinct versions.</p>

<p>That matters in rights talks because a conversation about “ebook rights” may really be a conversation about several different deliverables.</p>

<p>Have a basic record of:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>existing ISBNs</li>
  <li>formats already released</li>
  <li>formats planned but not yet released</li>
  <li>DRM or platform constraints that create distinct versions</li>
  <li>whether audio or enhanced editions are included in the rights position</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">6) Check for hidden rights problems in the manuscript itself</h2>

<p>A clean contract is not enough if the underlying content carries permissions risk.</p>

<p>Pause on the following before translation outreach:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>unlicensed images or illustrations</li>
  <li>charts, tables, or maps sourced from third parties</li>
  <li>song lyrics</li>
  <li>long quoted passages</li>
  <li>embedded branded material</li>
  <li>estate-controlled material</li>
  <li>contributor work with unclear assignment terms</li>
</ul>

<p>Translation can expand exposure, so minor unresolved permissions problems can become bigger commercial problems later.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">7) Decide what needs counsel instead of improvisation</h2>

<p>You do not need a lawyer for every ordinary checklist item. You probably do need one when the answer changes the actual scope of rights.</p>

<p>Escalate when you are dealing with:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>conflicting contract language</li>
  <li>unclear reversion status</li>
  <li>inherited rights or estate issues</li>
  <li>multiple contributors with different grants</li>
  <li>moral-rights questions</li>
  <li>tax or VAT implications tied to territories</li>
  <li>dispute risk across jurisdictions</li>
  <li>requests for broad adaptation or sublicensing rights</li>
</ul>

<p>That is the line between practical preparation and legal interpretation.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">8) Bring one concise rights sheet to every meeting</h2>

<p>If you only do one thing from this list, do this.</p>

<p>Create a one-page rights sheet covering:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>title</li>
  <li>author</li>
  <li>original language</li>
  <li>available languages</li>
  <li>available territories</li>
  <li>available formats</li>
  <li>current publication status</li>
  <li>sample availability</li>
  <li>contact and response time</li>
  <li>known restrictions or exclusions</li>
</ul>

<p>A strong rights sheet does not replace the contract. It makes the conversation legible.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The practical bottom line</h2>

<p>Before you spend time chasing fair meetings, email intros, or foreign-rights outreach, make sure your rights position is understandable to someone who has never seen your book before.</p>

<p>Clear ownership, clean scope, usable metadata, and honest caveats do more for translation-rights momentum than a bigger contact list.</p>
