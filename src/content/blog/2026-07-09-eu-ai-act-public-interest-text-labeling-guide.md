<p>The EU AI Act is easy to misread if your team uses generative AI anywhere in editorial work. The loud version says, "All AI text will need a label." The dismissive version says, "Human review solves everything." Neither is careful enough for a real publishing workflow.</p>

<p>What the European Commission has actually published is narrower and more useful. On its AI Act overview page, the Commission says certain AI-generated content should be clearly and visibly labelled, including <strong>text published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest</strong>. The same Commission materials say the transparency rules become applicable on <strong>2 August 2026</strong>. The underlying legal text in Article 50 says the same thing, while also preserving an important exception where the AI-generated text has undergone <strong>human review or editorial control</strong> and a natural or legal person holds <strong>editorial responsibility</strong> for publication.</p>

<p>For authors, editors, translators, and small publishing teams, that creates a practical deadline rather than an abstract policy debate. If you publish newsletters, explainers, market commentary, industry updates, or other public-facing informational text that may fall within this public-interest category, now is the time to map your workflow before <strong>Saturday, August 2, 2026</strong>.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Start with the two dates that matter</h2>

<p>The first date is <strong>10 June 2026</strong>. That is when the European Commission published the final <a href="https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/code-practice-ai-generated-content">Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content</a>, a voluntary support tool meant to help providers and deployers work toward Article 50 compliance.</p>

<p>The second date is <strong>2 August 2026</strong>. The Commission's <a href="https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai">AI Act page</a> says that is when the transparency rules become applicable. Its consultation page on draft transparency guidelines says the same date will govern obligations for providers and deployers, including duties tied to AI-generated publications on matters of public interest.</p>

<p>Those two dates do different jobs. The June code is support material. The August date is when the rule starts to matter operationally.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What the public-interest text rule actually says</h2>

<p>The legal hook is not every use of AI in publishing. Article 50 of the AI Act targets a narrower scenario: deployers of an AI system that generates or manipulates text <strong>published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest</strong> must disclose that the text was artificially generated or manipulated.</p>

<p>That phrasing matters. It points teams toward a workflow question before it points them toward a labeling widget.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>What is this text for?</strong> A private drafting aid is not the same thing as a public-facing informational publication.</li>
  <li><strong>Who is acting as deployer in the real workflow?</strong> The legal duty lands on the actor using the AI system in publication practice, not on vague "AI use" in the abstract.</li>
  <li><strong>Is the output actually being published to inform the public on a matter of public interest?</strong> That is closer to a compliance trigger than the simple fact that software assisted with drafting.</li>
</ul>

<p>The Commission's transparency consultation page is useful here because it says deployers will have to inform people when they are exposed to <strong>AI-generated publications on matters of public interest</strong>. That phrasing makes the publishing angle hard to ignore.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Why the human-review exception should not be treated as a loophole</h2>

<p>The same Article 50 paragraph also says the disclosure duty does not apply where the AI-generated content has undergone a process of <strong>human review or editorial control</strong> and where a natural or legal person holds <strong>editorial responsibility</strong> for the publication.</p>

<p>That is important, but it is not a free pass for superficial cleanup. The Commission's code page uses nearly the same wording when it describes the deployer-side obligations for public-interest text. The clean reading is operational: if a publisher wants to rely on that exception, it should be able to show that real review happened and that real editorial responsibility exists.</p>

<p>For small teams, the dangerous mistake would be assuming that a quick glance by a human automatically settles the issue. The law's wording points to a genuine editorial process, not cosmetic touch-up after a machine draft.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Name the accountable editor.</strong> If no person or entity clearly owns publication responsibility, the exception is harder to lean on with confidence.</li>
  <li><strong>Document the review step.</strong> Keep a simple internal record of who reviewed what, when, and at what level.</li>
  <li><strong>Separate assistive use from publishable output.</strong> Article 50 is about published text, not every internal brainstorm or summary.</li>
  <li><strong>Avoid pretending the guidelines are more settled than they are.</strong> The Commission is still using guidance tools to clarify scope and implementation questions.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">A practical checklist before August 2</h2>

<p>Most publishing teams do not need a giant AI compliance memo to handle this well. They need a short decision path.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Inventory your public-interest text formats.</strong> Check newsletters, rights explainers, market updates, blog analysis, press-facing summaries, and educational public copy.</li>
  <li><strong>Mark where generative AI enters the workflow.</strong> Drafting, restructuring, summarizing, translation support, and title or deck generation are not the same use case.</li>
  <li><strong>Decide which outputs will be labeled and which will rely on human review plus editorial responsibility.</strong> Do not leave that choice to improvisation on publish day.</li>
  <li><strong>Write a short internal standard now.</strong> A one-page rule is better than a vague promise that the team will "be transparent."</li>
  <li><strong>Keep the rule EU-scoped but commercially realistic.</strong> Even non-EU publishers may want one repeatable process if they serve EU readers or distribute there.</li>
</ul>

<p>This is also where our <a href="/blog/2026-07-02-ifrro-ai-licensing-and-rights-management-guide/">IFRRO AI licensing and rights-management guide</a> becomes a useful companion. That piece is about authorization, licensing, and rightsholder concerns around training and reuse. This AI Act issue is different. It is about transparency at the point of publication. Teams using generative AI in editorial workflows may need both conversations at once.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What not to overclaim</h2>

<p>Rex readers should keep three limits in view. First, this is an <strong>EU</strong> rule, even if its operational logic may influence wider publishing practice. Second, the Commission's support materials are helpful, but they do not erase every edge-case question about scope. Third, nothing in the current materials supports the lazy claim that every sentence touched by AI must always carry the same public notice in every publishing context.</p>

<p>The narrow practical truth is better than either hype or denial: if your team publishes text meant to inform the public on matters of public interest, and generative AI is materially involved in producing or manipulating that text, you should decide before <strong>2 August 2026</strong> whether the content will need disclosure or whether it will pass through a review structure strong enough to support the law's human-review and editorial-responsibility exception.</p>
