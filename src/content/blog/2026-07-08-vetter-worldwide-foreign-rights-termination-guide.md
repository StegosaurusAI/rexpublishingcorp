<p>Older publishing and music contracts often used very broad language about rights. The expensive mistake is assuming that broad language settled everything forever.</p>

<p>A January 12, 2026 ruling from the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit is worth attention because it pushes directly on that assumption. In <em>Vetter v. Resnik</em>, the court affirmed a judgment declaring the Vetter plaintiffs the sole owners of the copyright rights to the song at issue <strong>throughout the world</strong> after a termination under <strong>17 U.S.C. section 304(c)</strong>.</p>

<p>That does <strong>not</strong> mean every author can now reclaim every foreign right under every old contract. It does mean authors, heirs, and rights-facing publishing teams should read older worldwide grants more carefully, especially where U.S. copyright law and valid termination procedure may reopen rights questions that parties once treated as finished.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Why this ruling matters</h2>

<p>The operational issue is simple. Many older grants conveyed rights in sweeping terms, including rights throughout the world. When a termination right exists, the practical question is whether the author or successor is only recapturing U.S. rights or whether the recapture reaches the full bundle originally conveyed under U.S. law.</p>

<p>In this case, the Fifth Circuit said the district court's declaration was supported by the statute's text, context, and purpose. The opinion affirmed a ruling that the plaintiffs owned the relevant copyright rights to the work throughout the world. That is why the case matters beyond the song itself. It turns an abstract termination debate into a live rights-management question.</p>

<p>For Rex readers, the useful lesson is narrower than the headline. If an older deal granted worldwide rights under a qualifying U.S. copyright arrangement, the termination analysis may be more valuable than many authors or estates assumed. But the value only appears if the grant qualifies, the timing window is still available, and the notice process is handled correctly.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Keep the scope narrow: this is older-grant territory</h2>

<p>The U.S. Copyright Office's termination guidance is explicit that different statutory provisions apply to different grants. For grants executed by the author or specified successors <strong>before January 1, 1978</strong>, the relevant route is generally <strong>section 304(c)</strong>. That is the lane this article is about.</p>

<p>That distinction matters because loose summaries can turn a real legal development into bad workflow. The Vetter ruling is not a universal shortcut for every modern contract, every territory, or every dispute between an author and a publisher. It is a current appellate decision dated <strong>January 12, 2026</strong>, and it is most useful when read alongside the Copyright Office's own rules on eligibility, timing, service, and recordation.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The process rules are still strict</h2>

<p>The Copyright Office says termination rights operate inside a defined statutory window. The effective date of termination must fall within a <strong>five-year termination period</strong>. The notice must be served <strong>no less than two years and no more than ten years</strong> before that effective date. The notice also must be recorded with the Office <strong>before</strong> the effective date.</p>

<p>Those are not technical footnotes. They are the difference between a rights theory and a usable workflow.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Qualifying grant first:</strong> not every old contract can be terminated under the same section or by the same party.</li>
  <li><strong>Calendar discipline second:</strong> the five-year window and the two-to-ten-year service rule leave little room for drift.</li>
  <li><strong>Recordation third:</strong> the notice has to be recorded before the effective date, which means paperwork timing matters.</li>
  <li><strong>Evidence discipline throughout:</strong> teams need clean copies of the grant, chain-of-title context, and service records before they start making commercial claims.</li>
</ul>

<p>This is why termination work often breaks down in practice. People focus on the idea of recapture and underestimate the administrative burden required to make the position credible.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Recordation is necessary, but it does not settle the fight by itself</h2>

<p>The Copyright Office's Form TCS materials are unusually helpful on this point. The Office requires the cover sheet for notices submitted under sections <strong>203</strong> or <strong>304(c)</strong>. But it also says that recording a notice is <strong>not</strong> a determination of the notice's validity or legal effect.</p>

<p>That warning deserves more attention than it usually gets. Some teams treat recordation as if it were a final government confirmation of ownership. It is not. Recordation is part of the official public-record workflow. It helps establish and index the notice. It does not eliminate disputes about whether the notice was timely, whether the grant qualified, whether service was proper, or whether the claimed scope of recapture will hold if challenged.</p>

<p>In other words, recordation is mandatory process, not automatic victory.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What authors, heirs, and rights teams should do now</h2>

<p>If you work with an older grant that conveyed broad territorial rights, the practical question is not whether the Vetter ruling sounds encouraging. The practical question is whether your paperwork supports a disciplined review.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Pull the original grant language.</strong> Confirm what was actually conveyed, including any worldwide or renewal-rights language.</li>
  <li><strong>Map the statute before the strategy.</strong> Determine whether the grant belongs in a pre-1978 termination framework and who has standing to act.</li>
  <li><strong>Build the calendar early.</strong> If a termination window may still exist, calculate the effective-date range and service deadlines before commercial negotiations restart.</li>
  <li><strong>Separate recordation from ultimate validity.</strong> Filing discipline matters, but it is not the same as a final adjudication of ownership.</li>
  <li><strong>Avoid casual foreign-rights assumptions.</strong> The Vetter ruling may strengthen some positions, but it does not erase contract-specific analysis.</li>
</ul>

<p>That last point matters for publishers as much as for authors. If a backlist work has licensing value abroad, old assumptions about territorial control may be less stable than the catalogue history suggests.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The practical takeaway for Rex readers</h2>

<p>The Fifth Circuit's January 12, 2026 ruling is useful because it suggests that some older worldwide grants governed by U.S. copyright law may be terminated more fully than many market participants expected. But the ruling is only one part of the picture. The Copyright Office's own materials still point to the same operational truth: <strong>termination rights are only as useful as the timing, service, and recordation work behind them</strong>.</p>
