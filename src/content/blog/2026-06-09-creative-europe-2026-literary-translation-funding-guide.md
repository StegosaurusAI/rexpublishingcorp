<p>If you work in translation rights, the useful question is not whether Creative Europe exists. It is what the 2026 literary-translation call actually covers, who can use it, and what the latest results say about real market activity.</p>

<p>The European Commission’s Creative Europe programme says its “Circulation of European literary works” scheme supports the <a href="https://culture.ec.europa.eu/cultural-and-creative-sectors/books-and-publishing/circulation-of-european-literary-works">translation, publication, distribution, and promotion of works of fiction</a>. For publishers, rights teams, and translators, that matters because it links rights planning to a live public funding route instead of leaving every cross-border project to private guesswork.</p>

<p>Here is the practical read on the 2026 call and the most recent published results.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What the scheme is built to fund</h2>

<p>The Commission describes the scheme as support for the transnational circulation of European fiction. On its programme page, it says the action supports about 40 projects per year and roughly 500 literary works translated from and into at least 40 languages.</p>

<p>That scale matters for two reasons:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>it is large enough to influence which books cross borders at all</li>
  <li>it rewards organisations that can connect editorial choices with distribution and promotion, not just translation alone</li>
</ul>

<p>The same programme page also says the scheme is meant to strengthen linguistic diversity, including works written in less widely spoken languages, while raising the profile of literary translators.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Who can apply, and who cannot</h2>

<p>The safest high-level summary is simple: this is an organisation-level funding route, not an individual-author grant.</p>

<p>The Commission says organisations active in the publishing and book sector are eligible to apply, while individuals cannot apply on their own. The 2026 call notice adds that eligible applicants can apply either individually or as a consortium of at least two eligible organisations.</p>

<p>That distinction is easy to blur in casual conversation, but it changes planning. An author may benefit from a supported translation project, yet the operational applicant is the publishing or book-sector organisation behind the project.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">How the 2026 project sizes work</h2>

<p>According to the Commission’s <a href="https://culture.ec.europa.eu/whats-new/news/creative-europe-to-support-around-40-projects-to-boost-literary-translation-in-2026">15 October 2025 call notice</a>, the 2026 call carried a €5 million budget and a 29 January 2026 application deadline.</p>

<p>The same notice breaks proposals into three size bands:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Small scale:</strong> at least 5 eligible translations</li>
  <li><strong>Medium scale:</strong> at least 11 eligible translations</li>
  <li><strong>Large scale:</strong> at least 21 eligible translations, for consortia only</li>
</ul>

<p>That is the part rights teams should pay attention to. The scheme is not framed as one-book opportunism. It favors a portfolio view: multiple works, a coherent editorial rationale, and a distribution plan that can travel across languages and territories.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What the latest results say about actual demand</h2>

<p>The newest Commission results page, published <a href="https://culture.ec.europa.eu/whats-new/news/46-projects-selected-for-funding-under-the-2025-circulation-of-european-literary-works-call">19 March 2026</a>, gives a more concrete signal than programme language alone.</p>

<p>According to that notice:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>46 proposals were selected from 189 submissions</li>
  <li>more than €5.5 million was awarded</li>
  <li>499 works of fiction were chosen for translation</li>
  <li>those works will be translated from 36 languages into 24 languages</li>
</ul>

<p>That does not tell you which future project will win. It does tell you the programme is producing real translation volume, not just policy messaging. For authors and rights holders, it is one of the clearer public indicators that multilingual fiction circulation is being financed at a meaningful scale.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What this means for publishers and rights teams</h2>

<p>If you are planning foreign-rights outreach, the practical lesson is to package rights and editorial planning together.</p>

<p>The 2026 call language points to three operating realities:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>translation is only one part of the funded workflow; publication, distribution, and promotion also matter</li>
  <li>project size matters, so rights availability across multiple titles can be strategically important</li>
  <li>less widely translated languages remain a meaningful part of the programme’s policy logic</li>
</ul>

<p>In plain terms: if a publisher or rights manager wants to use this kind of funding well, they need a cleaner rights picture, a stronger title group, and a realistic route to market after the translation is finished.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What not to overclaim</h2>

<p>There are a few easy mistakes to avoid.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>Do not describe this as a direct funding route for individual authors.</li>
  <li>Do not assume every European country participates on identical terms without checking current programme participation details.</li>
  <li>Do not treat selection counts as a promise of easy odds; the latest published round still involved 189 submissions for 46 selected projects.</li>
</ul>

<p>That is why this scheme works better as a planning signal than as a guarantee.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The practical bottom line</h2>

<p>Creative Europe’s 2026 literary-translation call is most useful when you read it as infrastructure for cross-border publishing. It supports fiction translation at scale, but it expects applicants to think beyond the manuscript and into editorial strategy, circulation, and market follow-through.</p>

<p>For rights holders, translators, and small publishing teams, that makes it worth tracking even if you are not the direct applicant yourself. It is one of the clearest official signals of where multilingual book circulation is being actively backed.</p>
