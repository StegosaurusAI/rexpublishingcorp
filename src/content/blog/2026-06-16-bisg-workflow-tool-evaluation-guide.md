<p>Small publishing teams usually do not have a software problem in the abstract. They have a handoff problem.</p>

<p>An editor cannot see production status. Metadata lives in a spreadsheet no one trusts. Rights notes sit in email. Accessibility fixes get discovered too late. A new tool can help, but it can also add one more place where work gets lost.</p>

<p>That is why the framing from BISG’s <a href="https://www.bisg.org/events/how-to-evaluate-workflow-tools-practical-steps-for-small-publishers">How to Evaluate Workflow Tools: Practical Steps for Small Publishers</a> event page is useful. BISG says workflow-tool selection feels daunting for smaller organizations because the options are numerous, the trade-offs are not always obvious, and resources are limited. Its listed takeaways are refreshingly practical: define and prioritize requirements, compare options based on cost, integration, and usability, and plan for onboarding plus long-term adoption.</p>

<p>That is a better starting point than buying the most impressive demo.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Start with the workflow failure, not the vendor category</h2>

<p>BISG’s <a href="https://www.bisg.org/workflow-committee">Workflow Committee</a> says its role is to improve efficiency, document best practices, define workflow for the industry, and create resources that improve existing approaches. The useful implication for Rex readers is simple: tool evaluation should begin with the current workflow you are trying to repair.</p>

<p>Before comparing products, write down:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>which step currently breaks down</li>
  <li>who owns that step</li>
  <li>what information gets delayed, duplicated, or lost</li>
  <li>which downstream teams absorb the cost</li>
</ul>

<p>In publishing, that usually means mapping handoffs across editorial, production, metadata, rights, accessibility, and distribution. If a team cannot describe the current failure clearly, it is not ready to judge whether a tool actually fixes it.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Requirements first, features second</h2>

<p>BISG’s first takeaway is to define and prioritize requirements. That sounds obvious, but it is where many small teams drift into expensive wish lists.</p>

<p>A better split is:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Must-have requirements:</strong> the tool has to support the workflow you run now or the workflow you are realistically moving to soon.</li>
  <li><strong>Nice-to-have requirements:</strong> useful improvements that should not decide the purchase by themselves.</li>
  <li><strong>Future-watch requirements:</strong> things worth tracking, but not worth paying for immediately if the business case is weak.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a publishing team, must-haves might include version control for files, permissions by role, export reliability, metadata handoff support, or a clean way to track accessibility remediation. Those matter more than a long roadmap slide.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Compare tools on the boring factors that actually decide adoption</h2>

<p>BISG’s event page highlights cost, integration, and usability as core comparison criteria. That is exactly where many selections succeed or fail.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Cost:</strong> not just license price, but setup time, migration labor, training time, and the cost of parallel systems that never fully go away.</li>
  <li><strong>Integration:</strong> whether the tool can exchange clean information with the systems you already rely on for files, metadata, contracts, or distribution.</li>
  <li><strong>Usability:</strong> whether the actual staff doing the work can use it consistently under deadline pressure.</li>
</ul>

<p>Those tests are less glamorous than a feature comparison grid, but they are much closer to the real publishing question: will this tool reduce friction across the supply chain, or just move it?</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Onboarding is part of procurement, not a post-purchase surprise</h2>

<p>BISG’s third takeaway is the one teams often leave until too late: plan for onboarding and long-term adoption. A tool is not operational just because the contract is signed.</p>

<p>Before buying, teams should decide:</p>

<ol class="mb-6 list-decimal pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>who will own implementation</li>
  <li>which legacy process will be retired</li>
  <li>how staff will be trained</li>
  <li>what the first 30, 60, and 90 days should prove</li>
  <li>which metric will show that the tool is reducing rework or delays</li>
</ol>

<p>If none of that is clear, the risk is not just a bad purchase. It is a half-adopted system that leaves the old spreadsheet, inbox, or shared drive in place forever.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Why this matters now</h2>

<p>BISG’s February 2026 <a href="https://www.bisg.org/news/bisg-launches-book-industry-surveys-on-accessibility-readiness-and-workflow-pain-points">survey announcement on accessibility readiness and workflow pain points</a> says the organization is gathering evidence on friction in creating, delivering, receiving, and supporting accessible digital products, and on where guidance, tools, or coordination could have the greatest impact.</p>

<p>That matters beyond accessibility. It is a reminder that workflow breakdowns are not theoretical. They show up in late metadata, missed rights details, inaccessible files, duplicated manual entry, and avoidable cleanup work across the chain.</p>

<p>The BISG <a href="https://www.bisg.org/workflow-resources">Workflow Resources</a> page also makes clear that the committee treats workflow improvement as an ongoing resource problem, not a one-time software decision. The list is still a work in progress, which is a fair reflection of the real job: selection discipline matters because workflows keep changing.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">A practical scorecard for Rex readers</h2>

<p>If your team is evaluating workflow tools now, use a short scorecard before you sit through more demos:</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Problem fit:</strong> does the tool solve a specific workflow failure we can name?</li>
  <li><strong>Role fit:</strong> will editorial, production, metadata, rights, and accessibility staff actually use it?</li>
  <li><strong>System fit:</strong> can it exchange data with the systems we cannot replace?</li>
  <li><strong>Cost fit:</strong> what is the real first-year cost including migration and training?</li>
  <li><strong>Adoption fit:</strong> who owns implementation, and what process will stop once this goes live?</li>
</ul>

<p>If a candidate cannot pass that test, it is probably not a workflow solution. It is just another layer.</p>
