<p>Authors should not panic every time a message mentions copyright registration. They also should not assume that every email using the U.S. Copyright Office name is real.</p>

<p>That balance matters because two facts are true at once. On <strong>July 2, 2026</strong>, the Authors Guild's <a href="https://authorsguild.org/resource/publishing-scam-alerts/">Publishing Scam Alerts</a> page carried a warning titled <em>Writers Beware: U.S. Copyright Office Email Scam</em>. The Guild says some authors are receiving deceptive emails from someone impersonating the Office and asking recipients to verify their registrations. But the Office's own <a href="https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ19.pdf">Circular 19</a> also makes clear that real registration correspondence exists, usually arrives by email, and can carry deadlines that matter.</p>

<p>So the useful question is not "Should I trust this message?" or "Should I ignore it?" The useful question is: <strong>how do I verify it without creating a new problem</strong>?</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Start with the claim, not the panic</h2>

<p>The scam pattern described by the Authors Guild is specific. The message claims to come from the U.S. Copyright Office and asks the recipient to verify a registration. The Guild says those messages are fraudulent and do not come from the Office.</p>

<p>That does <strong>not</strong> mean every registration-related email is fake. Circular 19 says the Copyright Office sends most correspondence about registration claims by email from <code>cop-ad@loc.gov</code>. It also says general registration-related email replies are typically due within <strong>45 calendar days</strong>, and that a file may be closed if the applicant does not respond in time.</p>

<p>The operational lesson is simple: do not click or reply in a rush, but do not let a real deadline expire while you are deciding whether a message feels suspicious.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Use the Office's own verification path</h2>

<p>The best next step comes from the U.S. Copyright Office itself. Its <a href="https://www.copyright.gov/intellectual-property-toolkits/copyright-registration-toolkit.pdf">Copyright Registration Toolkit</a> says that if you are unsure whether an email is really from the Office, you can check with the <strong>Public Information Office</strong>.</p>

<p>That is a much cleaner workflow than arguing with a suspicious sender, forwarding personal documents to an address in the message, or assuming that any government-sounding language must be real. If the email references a pending registration, compare the claim in the message against your own filing records first, then confirm through the Copyright Office's official contact routes.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Pause before responding.</strong> Do not verify identity, registration numbers, or attachments through the email alone.</li>
  <li><strong>Check whether you actually have a pending or recent registration matter.</strong> A message is easier to judge if it connects to a filing you recognize.</li>
  <li><strong>Look for ordinary Office context.</strong> Circular 19 says most registration correspondence comes from <code>cop-ad@loc.gov</code>, but the safer move is still to confirm through official Office contacts.</li>
  <li><strong>Verify through the Public Information Office or the Office's official help channels.</strong> Use the Office's published contact routes, not links embedded in a suspicious message.</li>
  <li><strong>Track the deadline if the matter is genuine.</strong> Real correspondence can carry a 45-day reply window, and missed replies can lead to file closure.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Why this matters to ordinary working writers</h2>

<p>This is not a niche problem for large publishers. The Copyright Office's <a href="https://www.copyright.gov/engage/writers/">writer guidance</a> says literary works include books, essays, articles, blogs, and poetry, and it encourages writers to register their works. That means solo authors, journalists, essayists, bloggers, translators, and small publishing teams can all end up in the same correspondence lane.</p>

<p>In practice, scam pressure is most dangerous when authors already feel uncertain about the registration process. A message demanding fast action can look more plausible when someone has recently filed an application, paid a fee, or uploaded a deposit copy and is waiting for the next step.</p>

<p>That is why the cleanest defense is not paranoia. It is recordkeeping and channel discipline. If you know what you filed, when you filed it, and how the Office says it normally communicates, it becomes easier to separate a real workflow issue from an impersonation attempt.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The practical takeaway for Rex readers</h2>

<p>The Authors Guild alert is useful because it tells authors that a real impersonation pattern is circulating. The Copyright Office guidance is useful because it tells authors what legitimate communication and reply timing can look like. Put together, they support one practical rule: <strong>verify first, then respond through a trusted path</strong>.</p>

<p>If a message claims to be from the U.S. Copyright Office, do not treat fear as proof and do not treat silence as safety. Confirm the matter through official Office channels, keep an eye on genuine deadlines, and make sure your registration records are organized enough to check a claim quickly.
