<p>Europe’s book market grew in 2024, but not in the simple way a topline revenue figure might suggest.</p>

<p>According to the <a href="https://fep-fee.eu/European-Book-Publishing-Statistics-2024">Federation of European Publishers’ 2024 statistics</a>, European publishers generated about €24.9 billion in net annual sales revenue, up about 2.2% from 2023. That is the headline number. The more useful reading for authors, translators, and rights holders is the warning attached to it: FEP says that in real terms, after inflation, turnover has still lost more than 30% over the longer comparison period it tracks.</p>

<p>That distinction matters because a market can post nominal growth while still feeling operationally tighter. Higher prices can lift revenue even as unit demand softens, margins stay under pressure, and buyers become more selective about what they acquire, translate, or adapt.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What FEP is actually counting</h2>

<p>FEP is careful about definitions, and Rex readers should be too. The federation says its main figure covers <strong>net publishers’ turnover</strong>: publishers’ revenue from book sales, not the full retail market. It separately estimates total market value at about €36.1 billion and says total consumer expenditure was roughly €37 billion to €39 billion.</p>

<p>Just as important, FEP says its turnover figure <strong>does not include rights-sales revenue</strong> from translation deals, audiovisual adaptation, and similar licensing activity. So if you work in rights, the report is a useful market baseline, but not a complete picture of how value travels through publishing.</p>

<p>That limitation is not a flaw. It is a reminder to read the numbers honestly.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The volume signal is weaker than the revenue signal</h2>

<p>FEP estimates that around 2.5 billion copies were sold in Europe in 2024. It also says the trend in recent years shows a decline in sales volume.</p>

<p>For practical planning, that may be the most important line in the release. If volume is softening while nominal turnover rises, publishers and rights teams have to assume that not every category is benefiting equally from price-led growth. In that environment, a project usually needs a clearer reason to travel across territories or formats.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li><strong>Authors and agents</strong> may need sharper positioning before pitching translation or sublicensing opportunities.</li>
  <li><strong>Rights teams</strong> may need cleaner comparables when deciding which territories are still worth sustained outreach.</li>
  <li><strong>Format planners</strong> should be careful about treating market-size headlines as proof of broad-based demand.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">The biggest markets still matter, but they are not the whole story</h2>

<p>FEP identifies Germany, the UK, France, Spain, and Italy as the largest European markets by publishers’ turnover. That is useful context for anyone prioritizing rights outreach, co-edition conversations, or partnership research. Large markets still anchor a lot of practical decision-making.</p>

<p>But the report is more useful as a directional market map than as a shortcut. A big market can still be difficult to enter, and a smaller market can matter strategically if it has a strong translation culture, a targeted grant environment, or a better fit for a particular format or subject area.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">Digital growth is real, but unevenly measured</h2>

<p>FEP says ebooks were about 13% of the total market in 2024. It says audiobooks reached 4.2%, while also warning that the audiobook figure is likely underestimated.</p>

<p>The practical implication is not that every list should rush into audio or assume ebook growth solves market pressure. It is that digital format planning still needs local evidence.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>Ebook share is meaningful enough to matter in rights and pricing conversations.</li>
  <li>Audiobook momentum is real, but the data is still incomplete.</li>
  <li>Backlist, metadata, and territory-specific licensing terms matter more when digital performance is unevenly captured.</li>
</ul>

<p>That is especially relevant for publishers and authors thinking about adaptation sequencing: print first, digital first, or simultaneous release. A regional headline does not remove the need for title-level judgment.</p>

<h2 class="mt-10 mb-4 text-2xl font-bold text-gray-900">What Rex readers should take from the report</h2>

<p>The cleanest takeaway is that Europe’s publishing market remains large, active, and commercially significant, but the healthy-looking revenue line should not be read as proof of broad comfort.</p>

<p>FEP’s own caveats point to a more restrained interpretation: nominal turnover is up, inflation still distorts the picture, volume has softened, and rights revenue sits outside the core turnover number. For authors, translators, and rights holders, that means better planning usually starts with more precise questions.</p>

<ul class="mb-6 list-disc pl-6 space-y-2">
  <li>Which territories still justify active rights outreach?</li>
  <li>Which formats deserve adaptation investment now rather than later?</li>
  <li>Which revenue claims are volume-led, and which are mostly price-led?</li>
</ul>

<p>Those are better strategy questions than simply asking whether the market is up.</p>
